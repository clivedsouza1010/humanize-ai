Urban stormwater runoff is a leading cause of combined sewer overflows, and green roofs have been proposed as a way to reduce peak flows in dense cities. However, field evidence on their performance across varied climates and storm intensities remains limited. We monitored runoff from 24 extensive green roofs and 12 conventional roofs across four sites in the northeastern United States over two full years, recording more than 310 rainfall events. Green roofs retained a median of 54% of seasonal rainfall volume, with retention ranging from 78% in summer to 29% in winter. Peak runoff rates were reduced by a median of 61% for storms under 25 mm, but this benefit declined sharply for events exceeding 50 mm, where reductions averaged only 18%. Substrate depth and antecedent moisture were the strongest predictors of retention in a mixed-effects model, whereas vegetation type had a smaller and less consistent effect. Roofs with substrate depths of 15 cm or greater retained significantly more water than those with 8 cm (p < 0.01). These results suggest that green roofs can meaningfully reduce runoff from frequent, moderate storms but offer limited protection during extreme events. We recommend that stormwater planners treat green roofs as a complement to, rather than a replacement for, conventional drainage infrastructure, and that design standards account for seasonal variation in performance.
